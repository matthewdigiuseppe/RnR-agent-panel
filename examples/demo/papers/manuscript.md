# Fiscal Consolidation and Incumbent Support: Evidence from European Municipalities, 2008-2019

## Abstract

Do voters punish incumbents for austerity? We exploit a discontinuity in a
national grant formula that assigned sharply different consolidation
requirements to otherwise similar municipalities. Using a difference-in-differences
design across 2,412 municipalities in four countries, we find that incumbent
vote share falls by 3.8 percentage points (SE 1.1) in municipalities required to
consolidate. The effect is concentrated among municipalities with high
pre-crisis public employment, which we interpret as evidence that the electoral
cost of austerity operates through the visibility of service cuts rather than
through aggregate economic conditions. The results suggest that the political
costs of consolidation are contingent on how austerity is delivered.

## 1. Introduction

The political economy of austerity has generated a large literature with
conflicting findings. Some studies report substantial electoral punishment for
consolidation; others find none. We argue that this disagreement reflects
heterogeneity in how consolidation is implemented rather than genuine
inconsistency. Where consolidation is delivered through visible cuts to local
services, voters attribute responsibility to incumbents. Where it is delivered
through transfers and accounting adjustments, they do not.

## 2. Theory and Scope Conditions

Our argument rests on attribution. Voters punish incumbents when three
conditions hold: the policy change is salient, responsibility is clearly
assigned, and the voter experiences the consequence directly. Municipal service
provision satisfies all three in our setting. We would not expect the same
pattern where consolidation is achieved through national-level tax changes, or
where local incumbents can credibly blame a higher tier of government. We
therefore limit our claims to settings with substantial municipal fiscal
autonomy and clearly assigned local responsibility, which we document in
Section 3 and Table 1.

**Hypothesis 1.** Municipalities required to consolidate show lower incumbent
vote share in the subsequent election.

**Hypothesis 2.** The effect is larger where public employment is a larger share
of local employment, because service cuts are more visible.

## 3. Research Design

The national grant formula assigned consolidation requirements on the basis of a
debt-to-revenue ratio measured in 2009. Municipalities above the threshold were
required to submit a consolidation plan; those below were not. We compare
electoral outcomes in treated and untreated municipalities before and after the
2011 reform, using a two-way fixed effects specification:

Y_it = a_i + b_t + d (Treated_i x Post_t) + X_it g + e_it

Standard errors are clustered at the municipality level. Table 1 reports
balance on pre-treatment covariates. Table 2 reports the main estimates.

Our estimand is the average treatment effect on the treated: the change in
incumbent vote share caused by the consolidation requirement, among
municipalities subject to it.

## 4. Data and Measurement

We combine municipal election returns, national statistical office employment
data, and the finance ministry's consolidation register. Incumbent vote share is
the share received by the party of the sitting mayor. Public employment share is
measured in 2008, before treatment assignment. Appendix Table A3 reports summary
statistics and Appendix A1 reports the event-study estimates by year.

## 5. Results

Table 2 shows a treatment effect of -3.8 points (SE 1.1, p < 0.01). The estimate
is stable to the inclusion of region-by-year fixed effects (column 3) and to
dropping the largest cities (column 4).

Table 3 splits the sample by pre-crisis public employment share. The effect is
-6.2 points (SE 1.6) in the top tercile and -1.1 points (SE 1.4) in the bottom
tercile. We take this as evidence for the visibility mechanism: austerity is
punished where citizens see the cuts, which is what our theory predicts.

We also examined heterogeneity by unemployment, mayoral tenure, party family,
region, and pre-crisis debt level. None of the other splits produced significant
differences.

## 6. External Validity

Our four countries share high municipal fiscal autonomy. The results should
travel to comparable settings; we do not claim they describe consolidation
politics in centralized states, and the estimates speak to municipal rather than
national elections.

## 7. Conclusion

Voters punish austerity when they can see it. The electoral cost of
consolidation is not a fixed parameter but a function of delivery. Governments
that consolidate through visible local service cuts pay a larger electoral price
than governments that consolidate through less visible instruments.
